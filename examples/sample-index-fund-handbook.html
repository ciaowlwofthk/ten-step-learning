1Five-perspective panel

Five kinds of people look at the same thing and often reach opposite conclusions. Each must give a position, three pieces of evidence, and the sentence "they know but usually do not say".

Simulated practitionerTen years on the front line, has managed client accounts
Position: dollar-cost averaging works, but nine out of ten people fail at "stopping when it falls". Behavior discipline, not product selection, decides the outcome.
Evidence: ① client redemption peaks almost always land near index lows; ② very few people manage to keep investing through three full cycles; ③ among loss complaints, most are not wrong purchases but stopping midway and chasing back at highs.
Hidden insight: the industry knows perfectly well that DCA's "effect" comes largely from replacing a timing decision with a discipline decision — not from DCA itself raising returns.
ResearcherAsset pricing and portfolio theory
Position: DCA does not change expected return; it only changes the path by which the return is realized.
Evidence: ① the expected value of buying in installments equals that of a lump sum; the difference is variance, not mean; ② DCA reduces the single-point risk of "buying at a high", at the cost of opportunity cost in a bull market; ③ the improvement from DCA correlates positively with the asset's volatility — the more volatile, the more the path differs.
Hidden insight: for a broad-based index that rises over the long run, a lump sum is statistically better — but it requires tolerating huge short-term drawdowns, which almost nobody can do. So "better" does not mean "executable".
SkepticSpecializes in dismantling sales rhetoric
Position: DCA has been packaged as "risk-free arbitrage", which is the biggest misdirection.
Evidence: ① marketing materials love the "smile curve", but that curve is drawn after the fact; ② DCA cannot avoid a long-term deterioration in the underlying asset's fundamentals; ③ "buy more as it falls" in a single market's long decline is simply adding to losses.
Hidden insight: many DCA promotions deliberately start just before a decline, which makes the backtest curve look best. Change the start point and the conclusion changes.
EconomistInstitutions and efficiency
Position: the value of index investing comes mainly from cost, not stock-picking skill.
Evidence: ① passive products structurally charge lower management fees than active ones; ② the share of active funds beating their benchmark after fees is persistently low; ③ the lower the trading frequency, the smaller the friction cost — a certain saving.
Hidden insight: "the market is broadly efficient" and "you should buy index funds" are two propositions. The former does not imply the latter is optimal for you — it only says "beating the market average is hard", not "you can tolerate the market average's volatility".
HistorianLong-cycle view
Position: every generation believes its own market is the exception.
Evidence: ① several markets have had flat periods lasting more than a decade; ② "this time is different" is the common language at every bubble top; ③ DCA's historical performance varies enormously across markets, depending on the market's long-term direction.
Hidden insight: every historical conclusion about DCA depends heavily on the chosen market and window. The same method, in another country or from another start point, can produce the opposite conclusion.

C · Points of consensus (no side objects)

  1. Cost (fees and friction) certainly eats into returns — the lower, the better.
  2. DCA's value lies mainly in behavioral constraint, not return enhancement.
  3. If the underlying asset is wrong, no entry method can save it.
  4. "Buy more as it falls" must set a survival premise for the asset; otherwise it is dangerous.
  5. Backtest conclusions mean nothing outside their specific window.

D · Industry blind spots (almost nobody raises them)

  1. The stop-investing rate is the real decider. Every discussion compares strategies, yet few count "how many people actually invest through the full cycle".
  2. DCA's psychological cost is systematically underestimated. Keeping deductions going through two years of floating losses squeezes ordinary household cash flow and mood — rarely quantified into "strategy quality".
  3. Exit rules are almost always missing. Buying rules are debated endlessly; selling rules get one sentence: "hold long term".
  4. The cost difference between on-exchange and off-exchange paths is ignored. For the same asset, fees and operational friction differ, and many people have never done the math.

360-second briefing

A paragraph you can read aloud, no jargon.

Script
Many people think DCA is a technique that raises returns. It is not. If you put a sum of money in over twelve months, or all at once on day one, the mathematically expected outcome is the same — the difference is the volatility path in between. The benefit of buying in installments is that you will not happen to buy on the unluckiest day; the cost is that if the market rises all the way, you earn a little less.

So why is DCA still useful? Because it turns "when to buy" — a question you cannot guess — into "a fixed deduction every month" — an action you can perform. Many people lose money not because they bought the wrong thing, but because they stopped when the pain was worst, then chased back in after the rebound. That is what DCA actually governs.

So it has three premises: first, what you buy must exist for the long run — broad-based indices qualify, a single sector may not; second, you must actually finish investing — stopping midway means the design never existed; third, you must decide in advance when to sell — almost nobody does this step, and it ends as "cannot bear to sell when up, dare not sell when down".

5 key findings (in descending credibility)

  1. DCA does not raise expected return; it only changes the volatility path — a mathematical conclusion, not an opinion.
  2. Its real value is behavioral constraint: turning a timing problem into a discipline action.
  3. Asset selection is a precondition; no entry method can compensate for a wrong asset.
  4. "Buy more as it falls" holds only if the asset survives and rises long term; otherwise it accelerates losses.
  5. The missing exit rule is the most common practical hole — far more important than the buying rule.

8Quiz (2 of 10 shown)full version has 10

  1. ★ Regarding DCA vs a lump sum, the most accurate statement is:
    A. DCA has a higher expected return　B. DCA lowers the probability of loss　C. The expectations are equal; the difference is the volatility path　D. DCA always beats a lump sum
    Correct answer: C.
    Why A is wrong: equating "feels steadier" with "higher return". Linearity of expectation means installments do not change the mean.
    Why B is wrong: the probability of loss depends on the asset's final direction, not the entry method. Installments only spread entry points over time.
    Why D is wrong: treating one specific backtest window as a universal law — exactly the pseudo-proposition scored 42 in step 4.
  2. ★★★ Someone stops deductions after two years of floating losses, then adds a lump sum when the index returns to cost. The most accurate assessment is:
    A. Rational stop-loss　B. A typical behavioral bias that breaks the original strategy's structure　C. Lowering average cost, a good deal　D. Unrelated to DCA
    Correct answer: B.
    Why A is wrong: stop-loss logic applies to deteriorating fundamentals of an individual stock, not to short-term fluctuations of a broad-based index. Two different kinds of decisions.
    Why C is wrong: looking only at unit price, not timing — missing that the pause skipped the lowest-cost stretch.
    Why D is wrong: this is precisely DCA's core failure point — the strategy did not fail; execution was interrupted. This is the phenomenon behind the first "industry blind spot" in step 2.

9Feynman explanation · 12-year-old version

Told through a story about buying apples, without a single technical term.

Suppose you get 100 yuan of pocket money every week and want to buy apples. The price changes every week — sometimes 10 yuan per kg, sometimes 5.

Approach one: save up 1,000 yuan and buy all at once on some day. If you are unlucky and that day happens to be 10 yuan per kg, you get only 100 kg.

Approach two: buy 100 yuan's worth every week. At 10 yuan you get 10 kg; at 5 yuan you get 20 kg. After ten weeks you have spent 1,000 yuan but bought more than 100 kg — because you automatically bought more when it was cheap.

But the story has a second half: if the apple price rises all the way and never falls, then buying slowly every week actually loses compared with buying everything at the start — because you keep buying at higher prices. So the method works only if prices fluctuate and what you buy does not disappear.

Retelling check: if you can explain the following to someone else, you have passed this stage —

  1. Buying in installments and buying at once give roughly the same average price.
  2. The advantage of installments is that you automatically buy a bit more when it is cheap.
  3. But the thing must keep existing, and the price must rise and fall, for this to work.
  4. If it only rises, installments actually buy at a higher average price.
  5. The most likely failure is not the method but the person: unable to keep buying when it is cheap.

If you cannot explain it, that is fine. Tell me which sentence you got stuck on and I will retell it with a different story — the interaction gate reserved in step 9 of this skill.
